I spent the better part of a decade on the front lines of publishing's worst crisis. Between 2008 and 2013, I was building the first iOS apps for Time Magazine, Sports Illustrated, CNNMoney — sitting in the offices of premium publishers while the ground shifted beneath them. At Polar, we worked exclusively with media companies through the years when Google and Facebook methodically absorbed the advertising model that had funded journalism for a century.

I remember what it felt like in those rooms. Not just the fear — though there was plenty of that — but the paralysis. Everyone could see the numbers. Print advertising was collapsing. Mobile was exploding. Social platforms were inserting themselves between publishers and their readers, taking the audience relationship and the ad dollars with it. What nobody could see was the way out.

Most publishers never found one. But one did, and its story is worth telling right now, because I think we're watching the same movie again — this time with AI in the role that mobile and social played fifteen years ago.

## Twenty years to get back to even

In 2006, The New York Times Company generated roughly $3.3 billion in revenue. Advertising was about 60 percent of it. Then the financial crisis hit, the smartphone arrived, and by 2011 revenue had cratered to $1.56 billion. Less than half of peak. That's not a bad quarter. That's an existential event.

I reviewed the Times' performance regularly during my Polar years, and for a long stretch it was hard to see the turnaround in the numbers. From 2011 through 2016, revenue was flat — stuck around $1.5 to $1.6 billion — as digital subscription growth barely offset the continued bleeding of print ads. Five years of running hard to stand still.

Then it compounded. Digital revenue passed print in 2020. Digital subscriptions passed $1 billion in 2023. And in 2025, the Times reported $2.82 billion in total revenue — within striking distance of its pre-crisis peak, nearly 13 million subscribers, and a fundamentally healthier business than the one it lost.

Twenty years. That's how long the reckoning took. Sit with that.

## What they actually did

It's tempting to reduce the Times' comeback to "they launched a paywall." That misses everything interesting about it.

**They made the bet when everyone said it was crazy.** In 2011, the conventional wisdom was that information wanted to be free and readers would simply click over to the endless free alternatives. The Times bet the opposite: that people would pay for quality. Mark Thompson, who took over as CEO in 2012, went further and declared the Times a "subscription-first" company — reframing news as a premium product, more like Netflix than like a commodity feed.

**They had the guts to audit themselves.** The leaked 2014 Innovation Report is still one of the most brutal self-assessments I've ever read from a large company. It admitted, in plain language, that the Times was losing to BuzzFeed and Vox at distribution and packaging, and that its newsroom culture was built for a 6 PM print deadline in a world where readers peaked on their phones at 7 AM. They didn't bury the report. They restructured around it — audience teams, digital-first workflows, power shifted away from print editors.

**They anchored on what they actually did for people.** This is the part that stuck with me most. The Times figured out it wasn't in the newspaper business. It was in the daily habit business. Cooking. Games. Wirecutter. The Athletic. Wordle, acquired for pocket change and now part of millions of morning routines. By the end of 2025, roughly a third of their digital subscribers didn't pay for news at all. The asset was never newsprint — it was trust, craft, and a place in people's daily lives. Once they saw that clearly, they could experiment aggressively without losing the plot.

## The rhyme with AI

Publishers today are looking at AI the way they looked at Facebook in 2010. Answer engines are intermediating the reader relationship. Traffic referrals are eroding. The models are trained on the very content publishers produce.

But this time, the reckoning isn't confined to publishing. It's retail, where AI agents are starting to shop on behalf of customers and the storefront risks becoming invisible. It's SaaS, where a generation of software built on seat-based pricing and workflow lock-in is watching AI compress those workflows into a prompt. Anyone whose business sits between a customer and an outcome is feeling the same ground shift publishers felt fifteen years ago. The anxiety in the rooms is the same anxiety I remember from 2009 — and so, I suspect, is the paralysis.

But the print reckoning taught us what separates the survivors from the casualties, and I don't think the lessons have changed:

**A long-term view.** The Times' revenue was flat for five years after the paywall launched. Any leadership team managing quarter to quarter would have killed the strategy in year three. The winners of the AI transition will be the ones who can articulate where they'll be in 2035 and absorb a decade of awkward middle years to get there.

**The patience to see it through.** Strategy is cheap. Holding the line while the old business declines faster than the new one grows — that's the expensive part. Most companies flinch.

**The wherewithal to anchor on the customer.** Not the format, not the channel, not the legacy revenue line. The Times survived because it understood that its job was to be indispensable to a person's day — and news was just one way to do that. Every company staring down AI should be asking the same question: strip away how we deliver it, what do we actually do for our customers? That's the thing to protect. Everything else is up for grabs.

**The guts to rebuild from the ground up — using what you already have.** The Times didn't abandon its assets; it leveraged them. A century of brand trust became the foundation for a subscription bet. A legacy crossword became the seed of a games empire worth millions of subscriptions. The companies that win the AI era won't be the ones that torch their existing business, nor the ones that defend it to the death. They'll be the ones that treat their assets — data, trust, distribution, craft — as raw material for experiments, and lead with them.

The reckoning is coming either way — for media, for retail, for software, for anyone in between. It came for print first, and it took two decades for even the best-positioned player to claw back to where it started. If that's the timeline, the time to start changing yourself from the ground up isn't when the numbers turn. It's now.
